So now that I’ve convinced you to give cooking a try, the next big thing I’ll have to coax you into doing is meal planning. _Suddenly, colour-coded spread sheets flash before your eyes, words tumbling in and out of coloured blocks frantically as you try to create your grocery list in time… !_ Don’t worry. It’s nothing like that.

At least it doesn’t have to be. There are indeed apps you can use either on your phone or computer to plan out your meals and portions, plus no doubt you have that spreadsheet program you never use that came with your word processing program. Just me?... For some, this style of planning is perfect, for those like me however, it’s just stressful.

Instead, learn from my mistakes, and use a flexible, functional approach.

Before I share my blunders, let me outline my -now- successful approach. I get a $200 cash budget every two weeks for two adults (previously this was $160, then I got pregnant, so…). We usually eat roughly the same thing for breakfast every day, and lunches are either sandwiches or leftover dinners, so they require little planning and their ingredients are always on my shopping list. The day or two before I can take out the cash, I use sales as inspiration to decide on a few dinners and add their ingredients to the list accordingly. There's only three or for dinners to buy for, depending on what I’m cooking (since omelettes don’t make good leftovers, for example), leaving the list quite manageable. If I’m feeling really inspired, I might plan two weeks worth of meals in one go, but I only ever shop for one. Although it can suck to go grocery shopping on a weekend, it’s usually what happens just because payday is a Friday. If I’m really clever though, I’ve planned meals through to Saturday or Sunday so that I can shop Monday.

Back to those mistakes I made while formulating my approach:

**Mistake # 1: Cooking dinner every night.** BIG OOPS. There’s just too much food. Most recipes are for 4-6 servings (depending on how hungry you are), so there’s usually leftovers which is actually really useful for lunches, and —more importantly—not cooking every night. We’ve all got other things to do sometimes, right?

**Mistake # 2: Trying to cook something new (or complicated) every day.** If you’re learning about a new ingredient, you might not know how to use the whole thing. And if the meal doesn't turn out on top of that, you’ll have wasted even more time and money. Sticking with simple recipes avoids this too, but if you’re ambitious like I was you may still make this mistake. Building a repertoire staple meals you are familiar with will save time, and hopefully money. Even as a cooking enthusiast, I limit myself to one new recipe a week.

**Mistake # 3: Not making it a habit.** Going to the grocery store and just buying whatever looks good is loads of fun, but you’ll just end up spending more time later trying to figure out what to do with the stuff, _and_ you’ll still end up with something you don’t use that might not keep. Saving money means cutting out waste. Avoid the temptation: plan ahead.

A note on cooking marathons: while I would generally recommend cooking regularly, I recognize this isn't going to work for everyone. To be perfectly honest, I rarely prepare one lunch at a time, so I guess I do a little marathoning here and there as well (just the cooking variety though). You can cook your week's meals all at once if this works better for you, either portioning them out by meal if you're going to be on-the-go with them, or leaving them in containers to portion out when you're home. It will limit a little how and what you cook in some regards, but it's still very doable, both for budget and time management. Just pick the day of the week that works best for you, make sure you have all your groceries that day, and cook away!

As an extra suggestion, you might want to mount a chalk or white board somewhere near the cooking area to help you track all of this. I have one, though it's more to inform my husband what I have planned. I found that having the planned meals listed on the back of my super-low-tech paper grocery list helpful in reminding me what parts of my list are crucial and which are pantry stock-ups which keeps me in line while shopping.
