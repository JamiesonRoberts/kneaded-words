The last part of my approach to food budgeting left to elaborate is “buying smart”. In my first post, I simply referred to price matching and using flyers, but more can fall under buying smart too. Heck, just meal planning so you don’t buy too much is buying smart.

But first, that price matching. A certain local retailer has changed their policy, casting some confusion over how price matching ought to work, but in essence, you bring in a competitor’s flyer (or use an app like Flipp to display it) and the cashier adjusts the price to match it on the product you want to buy. The speed at which this can occur is just as much as dependant on the cashier as it is on you being organized with your groceries as you put them on the conveyor. Don’t feel bad, no matter how busy the lineup: the amount saved can really add up and as such is totally worth it.

For the locals: I’ve had the best price matching experiences at FreshCo and SuperStore. The former is great because it even shows you how much you saved, including sales, at the end of your receipt. The latter, while fast and offering good products, can be a bit of a temptation as SuperStore does charge a bit more for groceries, so you have to be careful shopping there (I always spend more there).

Another way to “buy smart” is to be careful picking out your produce. Now, I find produce to be the least expensive part of my grocery bill most of the time, but [prices are on the rise](http://www.cbc.ca/beta/news/canada/british-columbia/fresh-vegetable-prices-bc-statistics-canada-loonie-1.3292592). As such, spoilage waste is something to consider while shopping. Selecting produce can be a bit of an art: sometimes successful and sometimes not. I actually wrote a whole post on it, but I won’t bore you with it unless you really want me to. Basically: buy the freshest you can find, unless you plan on cooking it that day or the next. Leafy greens should be crisp, green and bouncy, and vegetables and fruits should be firm and without wrinkles. If you’re from Ontario, like me, you are living in a thriving, beating heart of farmland, so getting fresh seasonal produce for most of the year should be easy.

The newest snag to buying smart that I’ve found myself falling into lately is points. Those damned PC points. Load up some deals, buy what they tell you, get points, which adds up to grocery store credit. If you’re like me, you need to remind yourself to ONLY buy what you need, and to try to buy the recommended products only if they’re on sale, or at a good price that week. Falling for the “oh well I get points for it” trap can result in buying more than you need, or simply things you don’t need at all. Just don’t try to make it a competition of how many points to earn each week.

Lastly, when you’re planning out your meals, use flyers. It seems like a nuisance, but buying what’s on special that week will save you a few dollars each time. It’s also an easy source of “inspiration” when you can look to see what’s on sale then try to decide what to make with it. Just over a week ago I bought and cooked a pork loin for the first time because it was on sale: learned a new meal and saved a bit on groceries while I was at it.

For the locals: I just want to acknowledge that I do not shop at the dollar store or Giant Tiger but you can find great deals there. There’s too much other junk at the dollar store for me to enjoy shopping there, and Giant Tiger is simply too far out of my way. Likewise, Food Basics looks like it has some excellent prices, but I simply don’t live near one.

Words of warning: just because something is on sale, doesn’t mean you should buy it. You may already have enough stocked away in the pantry, or it may be something you don’t even need at all (i.e. it’s not healthy). Having a meal plan going in is one way to avoid this temptation, but just being aware is important too.

On buying in bulk: Stores that offer bulk packs of product like Costco, SuperStore, and Walmart can be great if you have a large family. Since I’m still only buying for two (and a half), I can’t speak much to how effective it is to shop there. It may be worth your time one week, however, to go through what you buy at these stores and calculate a price per volume or weight. Most other grocery stores actually list this on the pricing label, and often in a similar unit such as dollar per 100g or per 100mL, making it easier to compare brands. As such you can also use it to figuring out if you’re actually saving on that 24-pack of canned tomatoes…

If (or when) I get a Costco membership, I’ll try to do some sleuthing for you on what products are worth buying in large quantities!
