_“Anyone can cook!” - Chef Gusteau in Ratatouille (c Disney)_

If I can’t encourage you to cook then the jig is up, and this whole endeavour will be pointless, so this is goal number one. Believe me when I say there are _lots_ of people out there who believe as much as I do that anyone can cook. They’ll tell you the same things: it’s not as hard to learn and it’s not as time consuming as you think. If it gives you any hope, much like any other skill, practicing makes it easier. And this kind of practice comes with a reward: yummy food!

My addition to all the advice out there is to make cooking a habit. When you get home from work, it’s really tempting to just veg out on the couch in front of Netflix or your smartphone. Believe me, I’ve been there. But if you can force yourself into the kitchen to make dinner every other night—maybe even every third night to start—you’ll find it gets easier and easier. Meal planning will take it one step further in that you won’t even have to think about what to cook, you can just do it. They used to say it takes 30 days to form a habit, but apparently that’s [not true](http://www.huffingtonpost.com/james-clear/forming-new-habits_b_5104807.html). Still, a month seems like a reasonable amount of time so maybe start with that if you’re new to cooking; just don't give up until after two. Regardless, once you make it a habit, you won’t go back, no matter how tired. (Okay, some nights we give in and order sushi, but it’s once every ten days, not every day, or even every week).

While instructional videos and books are great tools, you don’t need to study them to cook a meal. I came across a great quote from [Julia Child](http://www.pbs.org/food/julia-child/): “The only real stumbling block is fear of failure. In cooking you've got to have a what-the-hell attitude.” I love that.

I asked my husband—who is always completely amazed at whatever I put in front of him—what parts of cooking mystify him. Between that and observations I’ve made through asking him to help out in the kitchen, I’ve come up with some basic pointers to give you that "what the hell" confidence:

1. **Pay attention.** Don’t walk away from oil heating or food cooking. Pretty much the only way you can make something inedible is by burning it to a crisp. (Or setting off the fire alarm; don’t worry, neither of us have done this).
2. **Read the recipe through all the way before you start.** Before you even buy the ingredients! Just trust me on this one.
3. **Keep it simple.** Start out by following simple recipes, using familiar combinations of food. What makes a recipe “easy”? Few steps and not too many ingredients.
4. **Think about the end result while chopping.** How is it going to be cooked and how long do you have to cook it? The smaller you cut it, the faster it will cook, so watch out! Also, if you’re cooking multiple things together, like in a stew, keep in mind that some things cook faster than others. Mostly, you learn this through trial and error. If you want to get fancy, you can also think about the texture you want of the end result.
5. **Measurements are usually just guidelines.** Unless you’re baking, you don’t need to stress about that tablespoon of olive oil being level or that cup of chopped carrots being full. Just read (or watch) a [Jamie Oliver recipe](http://www.jamieoliver.com/recipes/vegetables-recipes/pesto/#fDCfhV7DLYCLevzg.97) to see what I mean.
6. **Sharper knives are easier to use.** Kind of scary, you might think, but if your blades are sharp, they won’t slip, and you'll cry less when cutting onions. Just be mindful of where your fingers are at all times, use a cutting board that does _not_ slip around (use a damp towel underneath if it does), and tuck your fingers under, so your knuckles are closest the blade. Better skin than limb; or in this case, digit.

Phew! That was a long one. But how about a recipe where you can put those pointers into practice? It needs a name though… hmm, maybe _“Dad's Tinfoil Bundle of Vegetable Goodness”_? Yup. We’ll go with that. Pair it with some [BBQ](http://www.seriouseats.com/2012/06/hot-to-grill-boneless-skinless-chicken-breast.html) or [oven baked](http://www.gimmesomeoven.com/baked-chicken-breast/) chicken breasts and you’ve got dinner. And, by the way, if my recipes look long, it’s because there’s extra tips in there for you!

**Ingredients:** assortment of your favourite vegetables, such as: carrots, potatoes, celery, onions (optional), zucchini, maybe broccoli and some bell pepper too salt and pepper butter cheese whiz (or sour cream or grated cheese) green onion (optional)

**Instructions:** Preheat oven to maybe 400 F or get your BBQ going. Chop vegetables into sticks or slices ("[Julienne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julienning)" if you're feeling fancy). Cut the carrots so they're a little less than half the thickness of your finger, and the potatoes and celery a bit thicker. Onions you can slice as thin as you like or even chop them up fine (i.e. small). The zucchini will cook faster, so you’ll want to leave it in moderately thick slices, maybe just shy of 1cm, so that it doesn’t turn to mush. Likewise, broccoli and bell pepper will cook reasonably quickly, at least if you like them crisp like me, so leave them thick. Layer them in that order on a large, doubled rectangle of greased tinfoil (you can use your fingers and butter, or PAM type cooking spray), using a bit of salt and pepper between the onion and zucchini, but going easy on the salt if you're using salted butter. Dab with butter and cheese whiz (you could also use sour cream, or sprinkle grated cheese here if you'd rather), add some more pepper, and green onion if using. Take the long ends of the tinfoil and neatly fold them down together just twice; this is so you can check the vegetables later. Roll the short ends in as well to [make a pouch](http://www.dirtygourmet.com/campfire-cooking-how-to-make-a-foil-pouch/). Place on a baking sheet (just in case the pouch leaks or breaks) in oven or BBQ for I’d say ten minutes. Remember to set a timer if you’re the forgetful type. Being careful of escaping steam, gently ease the top open and poke the vegetables with a fork to test if they’re done to your likeness. When they are: order’s up! Enjoy.
